Eating in Japan with Dietary Restrictions: Dashi, Soy Sauce and Other Hidden Ingredients

Dashi is everywhere

Dashi (出汁) is Japanese soup stock, and it’s the foundation of Japanese cooking. The most common kinds are made from:

  • Katsuobushi (鰹節): dried, smoked bonito (a fish), shaved into flakes
  • Niboshi (煮干し): small dried sardines
  • Kombu (昆布): kelp, a seaweed
  • Shiitake (椎茸): dried mushrooms

Most everyday dashi uses bonito, often together with kombu. That means miso soup, udon and soba broth, simmered vegetables, the sauce on many rice bowls and even some “plain” side dishes usually contain fish, even when you can’t see or taste any. Instant dashi powder is common in restaurants too, and it usually contains fish extract.

Kombu and shiitake dashi are fully plant-based, but you have to ask for them specifically, and many restaurants simply don’t have them.

Soy sauce contains wheat

Regular Japanese soy sauce (醤油, shōyu) is brewed from soybeans and wheat. It’s in teriyaki sauce, ponzu, many salad dressings, marinades, simmered dishes, the dipping sauce for sushi, and the tare (sauce) on grilled skewers. Some soy sauces are made without wheat and say so on the label (小麦不使用, “no wheat used”), but a restaurant will normally use the regular kind.

Shared oil and shared water

Many restaurants fry everything in the same oil: tempura shrimp, breaded pork, vegetables. Some soba shops boil udon in the same water as soba. For a strict vegetarian, someone with celiac disease or a person with a severe allergy, this cross-contact matters.

Staff may not say “no”

Japanese service culture puts a lot of weight on being helpful and avoiding direct refusal. If you ask “Is there wheat in this?” a server may say “No” because they don’t know the recipe in detail, or may hesitate and say ちょっと… (chotto…, “it’s a little…”), which usually means “that’s difficult” or “I’m not sure.”

Treat hesitation as a “no.” The single most useful line on every card in this book is the last one: “If you’re not sure, please just tell me.” It gives staff permission to admit uncertainty, which is exactly what keeps you safe.

Restaurants aren’t required to label allergens

Japan has strict allergen labelling rules for packaged food (see our label guide), but restaurants are generally not required to display allergen information. Many chain restaurants publish an allergen chart (アレルギー表, arerugī-hyō) on their website or will show one if you ask. Small independent restaurants usually won’t have one.
