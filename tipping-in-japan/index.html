Tipping in Japan: Do You Tip? (And What Those Extra Charges on Your Bill Mean)

Short answer: no, you don’t tip in Japan. Not in restaurants, taxis, hotels or hair salons. The menu price already covers good service, which is simply expected.

If you leave cash on the table, staff will often chase you down the street to give it back. They aren’t offended, just confused. They think you forgot your change.

What to do instead of tipping

  • Say thank you. Gochisōsama deshita (ごちそうさまでした, “thank you for the meal”) when you leave a restaurant is the best “tip” you can give. Staff and chefs genuinely appreciate it.
  • Pay at the register. At most casual restaurants you take the bill to the register near the exit instead of paying at the table.
  • Put money in the tray. Many shops have a small tray (karuton) by the register. Place your cash there instead of handing it over directly.

The extra charges you may see on your bill

Tipping doesn’t exist, but some charges can surprise visitors.

Otoshi (お通し) or table charge

At many izakaya (Japanese pubs), you’ll get a small appetizer you didn’t order, and it will appear on your bill, usually a few hundred yen per person. This is the otoshi, a seat charge. It’s normal and not a scam (in Kansai it’s often called tsukidashi). You can ask about it when you sit down, but many places won’t remove it.

Service charge (サービス料)

Some higher-end restaurants and hotel restaurants add a service charge, often around 10%. This is the closest thing to a tip, and it’s added automatically. You don’t need to add anything on top.

Late-night charge (深夜料金)

Some bars and izakaya add a late-night surcharge (often around 10%) after about 10 p.m. Taxis charge about 20% more between 10 p.m. and 5 a.m.

Consumption tax (消費税)

Japan’s consumption tax is 10%, or 8% for takeaway food and non-alcoholic drinks (alcohol is always 10%). Since 2021, prices shown to customers must include tax (総額表示), so the menu price is usually what you pay. Look for 税込 (tax included). If you also see 税抜 or 税別 (tax excluded) next to a smaller number, the 税込 figure is the one you’ll pay.

Are there any exceptions?

  • Ryokan (traditional inns): Some Japanese guests give a small tip (kokorozuke), often ¥1,000–3,000, to the room attendant (nakai-san), usually at the start of their stay. This is optional, increasingly uncommon, and never expected from visitors. If you do it, put the money in a small envelope or wrap it in paper rather than handing over bare cash.
  • Private tour guides: Some guides who mostly work with foreign visitors are used to tips, but it’s still not expected. A thank-you is enough.

When in doubt, don’t tip. You’ll never offend anyone in Japan by not tipping.
