Vegan and Vegetarian in Japan: Hidden Fish Stock and What to Order

The main challenge is fish-based dashi and animal fats. Watch out for:

  • Miso soup: almost always made with fish dashi
  • Udon and soba broth: usually bonito dashi
  • Ramen: most broths are pork bone (豚骨, tonkotsu), chicken bone (鶏ガラ, torigara) or fish based. Look for restaurants that explicitly advertise vegan ramen.
  • Gyōza (dumplings): usually contain pork, and may be fried in lard
  • Tempura: the batter often contains egg, and the oil is shared with shrimp and fish
  • Okonomiyaki: the batter normally contains egg and often dashi, and it’s usually topped with bonito flakes
  • Desserts: gelatin (ゼラチン) in jellies and some puddings
  • Rice crackers and snacks: may be seasoned with fish or shrimp

Good options:

  • Shōjin ryōri (精進料理): traditional Buddhist temple cuisine, with no meat or fish. The dashi is often made from kombu or shiitake. Some temples, especially in Kyoto and on Mount Kōya (Kōyasan), serve it to visitors. If you are strictly vegan, ask ahead about eggs, dairy and any dashi.
  • Restaurants that label themselves vegan (ヴィーガン) or offer vegan menus, which have become much more common in large cities
  • Simple items: plain rice, tofu dishes made without dashi, vegetable sushi (kappa-maki with cucumber, oshinko-maki with pickles) with the soy sauce you trust
  • Convenience stores: check labels carefully (see our label guide). Plain rice balls (onigiri) with salt (塩) or pickled plum (梅) fillings are often simple, but check the ingredients for fish extract.

Note that “vegetarian” isn’t always understood the same way in Japan. Some people may think a dish with only a little fish stock or a few bonito flakes is “vegetable food.” Be specific: show the card.
